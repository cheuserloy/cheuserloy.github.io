The gut microbiota can profoundly influence the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), but the impact of bacteria residing within lung tumors remains largely unexplored. We show that bacteria colonizing mouse lung metastases hindered antitumor immunity by promoting regulatory T cell (Treg) differentiation and function through metabolites, including valine. Antibiotic inhalation, a strategy to achieve high airway concentration with limited effects on the gut, reduced Treg frequencies, expanding stem-like T cells, broadening the TCR repertoire, and boosting complete remission rates following PD-1 blockade. Replacing tumor-associated bacteria, transplanting Tregs, or depleting T cells dampened the efficacy of the anti-PD-1/antibiotics combination, whereas adoptive transfer of T cells from combination-treated mice into naive mice conferred protection against tumor challenge. DNA sequences consistent with tumor-associated bacteria in mouse metastases were detected in 33 out of 95 human lung cancer specimens, which exhibited increased Treg and reduced T cell infiltration. Our findings reveal that tumor-associated microbes represent a barrier to ICI therapy that can be removed by disrupting intratumoral bacterial dysregulation with aerosolized antibiotics.
